About the Cook

A Greek, a geek, and the small kitchen spirit who supervises both.

My story

I grew up between two worlds. In one, my yiayia's kitchen on a Greek island — tomatoes still warm from the sun, a pot of something always murmuring on the stove, and the firm belief that feeding people is the highest form of love. In the other, a CRT glow at 2am, a party of pixelated heroes, and the firm belief that one more quest wouldn't hurt.

For years I kept them separate. I cooked like my grandmother on Sundays and ate like a raid-night goblin the rest of the week — instant noodles, delivery apps, whatever could be consumed one-handed without looking away from the screen.

The Greek Geek is what happened when I finally merged the save files. Real food, mostly Mediterranean at heart, built for people who think in stats and systems: every recipe here has honest macros, a difficulty rating, and instructions written the way I wish patch notes were — precise, tested, and occasionally funny.

Why nerds deserve better food

Gamer food culture is a scam we ran on ourselves. Energy drinks and sad desk noodles aren't a personality; they're a debuff. The hobbies we love reward preparation, optimisation, and mastery — and cooking is exactly that kind of system. It has skill trees (knife work, heat control), loot (leftovers), and boss fights (anything involving filo pastry).

You don't need to become a chef. You need about ten reliable recipes, a sharp knife, and the realisation that a 20-minute stir-fry gives you better stats than anything a delivery app can deliver — for a third of the gold.

The lore of Miso

Every kitchen has a spirit. Mine showed up the night I attempted my grandmother's avgolemono from memory and — against every law of culinary physics — didn't curdle it. A small flame-shaped creature with a wooden spoon on its back has floated around my stove ever since.

Miso is my kitchen companion and this site's quality control. If a recipe is here, Miso has watched me cook it at least three times: once to figure it out, once to break it, and once to make sure the fix holds. Miso's own tastes run to anything simmered, anything with sesame, and rolling natural twenties on dessert.
